🧬 Our Evolutionary Story

The Stone Age lasted approximately 3.3 million years. During this time, humans ate wild animals, fish, organs, roots, tubers, nuts, berries, and seasonal fruits. Our bodies evolved for this.

📅 The Calendar Metaphor

If we compress 3.3 million years of human evolution into one calendar year — from January 1st to December 31st — we can see just how recent our dietary changes really are. The entire history of hunting and gathering spans from Day 1 through Day 364. Agriculture begins in the last 24 hours. Industrial seed oils appear in the last 20 minutes of December 31st.

🥩 Hunting & Gathering Days 1–364 (~3.3 million years)
🌾 Grain Agriculture Last ~24 hours (~10,000–12,000 years ago)
🏭 Industrial Seed Oils Last ~20 minutes (~1900, 1% → 85% of dietary fat)
💡

For 364 days of our evolutionary year, every human on Earth ate diverse whole food — wild animals, fish, organs, roots, nuts, and seasonal fruits. Grain-based diets are an extreme biological novelty.

Every aspect of human physiology developed in response to an animal-based whole-food diet over 3 million+ years. Our stomach acid pH is extremely low (pH 1.5–2), comparable to scavengers — ideal for breaking down meat and bone. Our bile production is robust and continuous, optimized for digesting animal fats. Our enzyme profiles are dominated by proteases and lipases — enzymes that digest protein and fat. Our intestinal length, jaw structure, and brain size all reflect a diet rich in nutrient-dense animal foods. These adaptations took millions of years to develop and cannot be undone by 10,000 years of agriculture, let alone 120 years of industrial food processing.

Bone Density Declined

Pre-agricultural human skeletons consistently show thicker, denser bones. After the transition to grain-based diets, bone density decreased significantly. This is attributed to reduced mineral intake (particularly calcium, magnesium, and zinc from animal sources), increased phytate consumption from grains which chelates minerals, and reduced physical activity patterns shifting from active hunting to sedentary farming.

Average Height Dropped

Hunter-gatherer populations averaged significantly taller stature than early agricultural populations. In some regions, average height dropped by 5–6 inches after the adoption of grain farming. This reflects reduced protein quality and quantity, lower micronutrient density, and increased disease burden. It took thousands of years for average height to partially recover — and in many populations, it never fully did until modern nutrition improved protein access.

Tooth Decay Became Widespread

Pre-agricultural humans had virtually no tooth decay. The shift to grain-based diets introduced high levels of fermentable carbohydrates that feed acid-producing oral bacteria. Archaeological studies consistently show dental caries rates below 2% in hunter-gatherers, rising to 10–20% or higher in early farming communities.

Jaw & Skull Structure Changed

Hunter-gatherer skulls show broad, well-developed jaws with straight teeth and no overbite. Post-agricultural skulls show narrowed jaws, crowded teeth, malocclusion, and the appearance of overbites. This is partly due to softer, processed foods requiring less chewing force (reducing jaw development) and partly due to nutritional deficiencies affecting craniofacial growth — particularly deficiencies in vitamins A, D, and K2 which are abundant in animal foods.

🏭 The Seed Oil Problem — Fat Chemistry

For all of human history, dietary fats came from animals and whole plants — butter, tallow, lard, olive oil. Industrial seed oils (soybean, canola, sunflower, corn, cottonseed) only entered the food supply around 1900. By the end of the 20th century, seed oils went from approximately 1% to 85% of added dietary fat. This is the most radical change to the human fat supply in our entire evolutionary history.

Saturated Fat

"If it's natural and solid at room temperature, it's most likely healthy. Saturated fats are solid at room temperature."

Why Seed Oils Are Specifically Problematic

  • Industrial extraction process: Hexane solvent extraction, high-heat pressing, chemical deodorization to mask rancidity — these are industrial chemical processes, not food preparation.
  • Already oxidized before consumption: The extraction process itself damages the polyunsaturated fats. By the time you consume them, they're already rancid — the deodorization step simply masks the smell and taste of spoilage.
  • Omega-6 overload: The ancestral omega-6 to omega-3 ratio was approximately 1:1. The modern diet, dominated by seed oils, pushes this ratio to 20:1 or higher — a dramatic pro-inflammatory shift.
  • Linoleic acid incorporation: Linoleic acid from seed oils incorporates directly into cell membranes, where it drives chronic systemic inflammation, disrupts cellular signaling, and creates oxidized lipid metabolites.
⚠️

Summary: Human biology was shaped by millions of years of whole, wild, nutrient-dense food. Skeletal and dental evidence from the agricultural transition shows weaker bones, shorter stature, smaller jaws, and widespread decay. Seed oils are the most recent and potentially most damaging addition to the human diet.

🌿 The Fibre Myth

One of the most entrenched beliefs in modern nutrition. The evidence does not support the certainty with which fibre is promoted.

What Fibre Actually Is

Fibre cannot be digested by the human body. This is a fundamental biochemical fact. Plant cell walls are built with beta-glycosidic bonds — and human digestive enzymes can only recognize and cleave alpha-glycosidic bonds. This isn't a minor limitation — it's a fundamental biochemical incompatibility.

There are two types: soluble fibre (which gut bacteria can partially metabolize, producing short-chain fatty acids) and insoluble fibre (which passes through the entire digestive tract essentially unchanged — bulk roughage). The claim that humans "need" a substance they cannot digest deserves scrutiny.

Gas, Bloating & Digestive Discomfort

Insoluble fibre passes undigested to the large intestine, where gut bacteria attempt to ferment it. This fermentation produces gases — hydrogen, methane, and carbon dioxide. The result: bloating, abdominal cramping, constipation, and diarrhea. For people with FODMAP sensitivity or irritable bowel conditions, high fibre intake can be actively harmful.

"Bloating, abdominal pain, and excess gas are all common consequences of increasing fiber intake." — Rekha Chaudhary, MD

Reduced Nutrient & Mineral Absorption

Fibre impairs mineral absorption through two distinct mechanisms. First, physical entrapment — nutrients become trapped within the fibre matrix and pass through the gut before they can be absorbed. Second, chemical binding — phytates and oxalates in fibre-rich foods form insoluble complexes with essential minerals, rendering them unavailable for absorption.

Research shows that high-fibre diets contained 3× more phytate and approximately 4× more oxalate than moderate-fibre diets — directly reducing mineral bioavailability.

☀️ Vitamin D3 — The Sun Hormone

Vitamin D3 is not actually a vitamin — it's a secosteroid hormone. When UVB radiation strikes your skin, it triggers the synthesis of cholecalciferol (vitamin D3). This hormone influences over 1,000 genes, modulates immune function, regulates calcium metabolism, affects mood and cognition, and supports muscle function.

You need a minimum of 30 minutes of direct sunlight daily with your legs exposed. Legs represent the largest continuous skin surface and are the most efficient site for vitamin D synthesis. Supplements are not equivalent to natural sun-derived D3 — the body's endogenous production includes regulatory feedback mechanisms that prevent toxicity, which oral supplementation bypasses.

🔬 Sun & Sugar Metabolism

UV exposure enhances insulin sensitivity and improves glucose metabolism independently of vitamin D levels. Nitric oxide released from sun-exposed skin improves blood vessel dilation and blood flow. Without adequate sun exposure, the pathway toward insulin resistance → metabolic syndrome → type 2 diabetes is accelerated. This is one reason why metabolic disease rates are highest in populations living farthest from the equator and spending the most time indoors.

🕐 Circadian Rhythm — Your Biological Clock

Every cell in your body runs on an approximately 24-hour circadian rhythm, synchronized primarily by light entering your eyes. Morning sunlight — containing the specific wavelengths of red, orange, and blue light present at sunrise — is the most powerful anchor for this system.

  • Cortisol timing: Healthy morning spike provides alertness and energy; circadian disruption flattens this curve
  • Melatonin regulation: Morning light exposure → stronger nighttime melatonin production → deeper, more restorative sleep
  • Mood & cognition: Light-driven serotonin production directly affects mental clarity and emotional stability
  • Metabolic synchronization: Meal timing, digestive enzyme secretion, fat metabolism — all regulated by circadian signals
"The single most impactful thing for sleep quality, energy, and metabolic health is getting outside within the first hour of waking and exposing your eyes to natural sunlight for 10–30 minutes. No sunglasses."

🌑 The Blue Light Plague

While morning sun anchors your rhythm, modern artificial light destroys it. Screens (phones, laptops, TVs) and LED bulbs emit high concentrations of blue light. This specific wavelength signals the brain that it is high noon, immediately suppressing melatonin production.

📱

The 2-Hour Rule: For multiple hours before bed, you must eliminate screen exposure. Even a few minutes of scrolling can delay melatonin release by hours, causing sleep quality to crater even if you manage to fall asleep.

☀️

Practical minimum: 30 minutes of direct sunlight daily with legs exposed. Morning sun within the first hour of waking. No sunglasses during morning light exposure. Your ancestors spent the entire day outdoors — 30 minutes is the absolute bare minimum.

💧 Water Quality

Ancestral humans drank from springs, rivers, and streams — mineral-rich and uncontaminated. Modern municipal water is a chemical cocktail.

⚠️ The Tap Water Problem

Modern municipal water contains chlorine, fluoride, microplastics, pharmaceutical residues, and heavy metals. If we are returning to ancestral inputs, water cannot be ignored. Pollution of water sources is a major underappreciated health threat.

  • Chlorine: Added to kill bacteria, but also disrupts your gut microbiome.
  • Fluoride: A neurotoxin added to water supplies. Accumulates in the pineal gland and bones.
  • Microplastics: Found in over 90% of bottled water and significant amounts of tap water.
  • Pharmaceuticals: Trace amounts of antibiotics, hormones, and antidepressants are routinely found in city water.

✅ The Solution

Spring Water: The gold standard. Naturally filtered by the earth, structured, and rich in essential minerals like magnesium and calcium.

Reverse Osmosis (RO): If spring water isn't accessible, use a high-quality Reverse Osmosis filter to remove contaminants. Crucial: You must add minerals (sea salt or electrolyte drops) back into RO water, as the filtration process strips everything.

🚫

Avoid Plastic Bottles: Plastic leaches xenoestrogens into the water, especially when heated. Use glass or stainless steel.

The Earth carries a mild negative charge. Your body accumulates a positive charge (free radicals, oxidative stress) through metabolism and modern exposures (EMFs, synthetic materials). Skin-to-ground contact transfers free electrons from the earth into your body.

🔥 Burned Food & Acrylamide

High-temperature cooking of starchy foods produces acrylamide. Understanding this reaction is critical for reducing toxic burden.

⚠️ The Acrylamide Problem

Acrylamide forms through the Maillard reaction when the amino acid asparagine reacts with reducing sugars at temperatures above approximately 120°C (248°F). The highest-acrylamide foods include:

  • French fries and potato chips
  • Toast and baked goods
  • Coffee (the roasting process burns the seeds, creating high acrylamide levels)
  • Breakfast cereals
  • Biscuits and crackers

Acrylamide is classified as an IARC Group 2A probable carcinogen. It's the same compound used industrially in water treatment and plastic manufacturing.

☕ The Caffeine & Coffee Trap

Caffeine does not provide energy; it is a neurotoxin that poisons the body into staying awake by blocking adenosine receptors. With a half-life of over 7 hours, a morning coffee ensures trace amounts are still disrupting your brain at bedtime.

Coffee is specifically problematic because the roasting process burns the seeds. Your body detects this "burned" profile as bitter—a natural warning signal for poison. This is why children and young people instinctively reject the taste; adults only "acquire" it through repeated exposure and desensitization.

Health Damage Pathways

  • Neurological: Confirmed neurotoxin causing peripheral neuropathy — numbness, tingling, muscle weakness in extremities. Occupational exposure studies confirm dose-dependent nerve damage.
  • Carcinogenic: Causes tumors in animal studies across multiple organs. Human epidemiological studies associate high acrylamide intake with increased risk of kidney, endometrial, and ovarian cancers.
  • Reproductive: Glycidamide (the primary metabolite of acrylamide in the body) binds directly to DNA, forming adducts that can cause mutations and chromosomal damage.

🛡️ Mitigation Strategies

  • Cook low and slow — lower temperatures produce dramatically less acrylamide
  • Boil or steam instead of frying — water-based cooking stays at 100°C, safely below the 120°C threshold
  • Soak potatoes 15–30 minutes before cooking — reduces acrylamide by up to 50%
  • Don't refrigerate potatoes — cold storage converts starch to sugar, increasing acrylamide precursors
  • Don't burn toast — golden, not brown or black
  • Choose animal foods — meat, fish, and eggs produce negligible acrylamide even at high temperatures because they contain minimal asparagine + reducing sugar combinations

🔬 Nutrient Destruction from High-Heat Cooking

  • Vitamin C: Destroyed above 70°C — one of the most heat-sensitive nutrients
  • B vitamins (B1, B5, B9): Water-soluble and heat-labile; significant losses during high-heat cooking
  • Vitamin E: Degraded by high heat and oxidation
  • Omega-3 fatty acids: Oxidize at high temperatures, forming toxic lipid peroxides
  • Enzymes: Denatured above 45–50°C — all enzymatic activity destroyed
"The irony: the same high-heat cooking that creates toxic compounds simultaneously destroys the beneficial nutrients."
🔥

Bottom line: Cook gently. Rare or medium-rare meat, soft-boiled eggs, gentle steaming. The darker and more charred the food, the more toxins created and fewer nutrients remaining. Animal foods cooked properly produce virtually no acrylamide.

🍺 Alcohol: The Ancestral Verdict

Ethanol is metabolized as a toxin. The liver prioritizes alcohol detoxification above all other metabolic processes — halting fat burning, protein synthesis, and nutrient processing until it is cleared.

  • Sleep Destroyer: Even small amounts suppress REM sleep and fragment sleep architecture.
  • Gut Damage: Increases intestinal permeability ("leaky gut") and depletes B vitamins and magnesium.
  • No Safe Dose: Fermentation is a post-agricultural invention. There is no ancestral precedent for regular alcohol consumption.

What the evidence and evolutionary logic point toward — practical steps to return to ancestral nutrition.

🚫 Eliminate Seed Oils Completely

This is the single most impactful thing you can do for your health. All of these must go: soybean oil, canola/rapeseed oil, sunflower oil, corn oil, safflower oil, cottonseed oil, grapeseed oil, rice bran oil. These oils are extracted using chemical solvents (hexane), subjected to high heat, and chemically deodorized to mask the rancidity that's already present.

Their high content of oxidized linoleic acid drives systemic inflammation at every level — from cell membranes to arterial walls.

Use ghee or tallow instead — heat-stable, nutrient-dense, ancestral cooking fats that don't oxidize into toxic aldehydes. Ghee has a smoke point of ~250°C. These are the original cooking fats; seed oils are the substitution, not the other way around.

The "treat" mentality is psychologically destructive — the threshold for what constitutes a "treat" shifts upward constantly. What starts as "occasionally" becomes "weekends" becomes "whenever I feel like it."

🦴 Bone Marrow & Bone Broth

Bone marrow was one of the earliest ancestral foods — tool marks on bones predate Homo sapiens. It is rich in fat-soluble vitamins, collagen precursors (glycine, proline), and healthy fats.

  • Glycine: Supports gut lining repair, liver detoxification, and sleep quality.
  • Collagen: The structural protein for joints, skin, hair, and connective tissue.

Bone Broth: Simmering bones for 24+ hours extracts these nutrients into a highly absorbable form. Treat this as essential alongside organ meats.

🥩 Meat Quality Hierarchy

A) Ruminant Animal Superiority

Beef, lamb, goat, and bison are ruminant animals — they possess a four-chamber stomach that functions as a sophisticated fermentation system.

This fermentation-based digestive system converts low-quality feed into high-quality, nutrient-dense meat. Even conventionally raised ruminant meat is significantly better than conventional chicken or pork because the four-chamber system effectively filters and converts input quality. Ruminant meat is always the top choice.

B) Non-Ruminant Conditional Use

Pork and chicken are only acceptable if sourced from verified high-quality local farms. These are single-stomach animals — what they eat directly becomes their tissue composition. Conventional chicken and pork are fed soy, corn, antibiotics, and growth promoters — all of which transfer directly into the meat you consume. If you cannot verify the farm and feed quality, avoid entirely.

C) Farmed Fish Warning

Most farmed salmon is fed soy-based feed pellets, resulting in skewed omega-6:omega-3 ratios, incomplete development, and lower vitamin content. Wild-caught is always preferable.

D) Raw vs. Cooked Optimization

🥩 Primary recommendation: completely raw — eliminates all cooking-related damage, preserves enzymes, vitamins, and fatty acid integrity in their natural state.

🔥 Secondary: Light cooking (rare/medium-rare) for those who prefer cooked food or are transitioning.

Heat denatures proteins (reducing their biological value), destroys heat-sensitive vitamins (C, B1, B5, B9, E), oxidizes fats creating harmful lipid peroxides, and kills beneficial enzymes that aid digestion. For a detailed explanation of why heavy cooking is harmful, see the Burned Food & Acrylamide section above.

Furthermore, on a low-inflammation animal-based diet, your requirement for vitamin C (an antioxidant) is significantly lower than on a high-oxidative-stress grain diet.

Blue Zone Populations

"Blue Zones eat mostly plants and live longest."
  • Misrepresented: They consume significant animal foods (fish, pork, cheese) as staples.

Red Meat & Cancer

"WHO/IARC classification of processed meat as carcinogenic."
  • Unreliable Data: Based on self-reported questionnaires prone to massive recall bias.
  • Healthy User Bias: Studies fail to isolate meat from seed oils and refined carbs.
  • Processing Risk: Actual risk comes from industrial additives and charring, not the meat itself.
🫀

Saturated Fat & Cholesterol

"Saturated fat clogs your arteries and raises cholesterol, leading to heart disease."
  • Failed Hypothesis: Decades of data and massive meta-analyses have repeatedly failed to prove a direct link between saturated fat intake and heart disease.
  • Particle Quality: Saturated fat raises "Large Fluffy" (Pattern A) LDL, which is benign; the dangerous "Small Dense" LDL is actually driven by sugar and seed oils.
  • Hormone Foundation: You want cholesterol; it is the mandatory building block for all steroid hormones. Without it, you cannot produce Testosterone (Male), Estrogen & Progesterone (Female), or Cortisol. These hormones are critically important for vitality, mood, and reproduction. Blaming cholesterol for heart disease is like blaming firemen for the fire they are trying to put out.
🌿

Mediterranean Diet

"RCTs show cardiovascular benefits from olive oil and plant-heavy traditional diets."
  • The Baseline Fallacy: Major trials (PREDIMED, Lyon Diet Heart) compare the Mediterranean diet to a standard Western diet. Beating a baseline of seed oils and processed sugar doesn't prove olive oil is optimal; it only proves it is less toxic than industrial waste.
  • Biological Feedback: High-quality EVOO is naturally bitter and peppery because of oleocanthal, which triggers the same irritant receptors as ibuprofen. This bitterness is a mild biological warning signal. Animal fats like ghee are immediately palatable and satisfying alone.
  • Omega-6 Burden: Olive oil contains ~8–10% linoleic acid (omega-6), while butter contains only ~2–3%. In a framework where excess linoleic acid drives systemic inflammation, animal fats remain the superior choice for cellular integrity.
  • Confounding Variables: Mediterranean health is driven by abundant sunlight, high activity, and low stress—not by the specific inclusion of plant oils.

  • Skewed Averages: Dragged down by high infant mortality and physical trauma, not diet.
  • Robust Adults: Survivors routinely reached 60–100+ with zero metabolic disease.

📖 Glossary

Definitions for all technical terms used throughout this guide.

LD50

Lethal Dose 50% — the dose of a substance required to kill 50% of a test population. The standard measure of acute toxicity. Lower LD50 = more toxic.

Bioavailability

The proportion of a nutrient that is absorbed from the diet and used for normal body functions. Animal-source nutrients generally have far higher bioavailability than plant-source equivalents.

Beta-glycosidic Bond

A chemical bond linking glucose molecules in a configuration that human digestive enzymes cannot recognize or break. Found in cellulose and other plant structural carbohydrates.

Alpha-glycosidic Bond

A chemical bond linking glucose molecules in a configuration that human digestive enzymes CAN recognize and break. Found in starch and glycogen.

Fermentation

Metabolic process where microorganisms break down organic substances. In the gut, bacterial fermentation of undigested fibre produces gases and short-chain fatty acids.

Phytates (Phytic Acid)

The storage form of phosphorus in plants. Strongly binds to minerals (iron, zinc, calcium, magnesium), forming insoluble complexes that the human body cannot absorb.

Oxalates

Organic acids found in many plants (spinach, rhubarb, beets) that bind calcium and other minerals, forming insoluble crystals. Can accumulate in kidneys as kidney stones.

Glycoalkaloids

Toxic nitrogen-containing compounds found in nightshade plants (potatoes, tomatoes, eggplant). Heat-stable — not fully destroyed by cooking. Include solanine and chaconine.

Lectins

Carbohydrate-binding proteins found in many plants, especially legumes and grains. Can bind to intestinal lining, cause agglutination of red blood cells, and trigger immune responses.

Heme Iron

The form of iron found in animal blood and muscle tissue. 15-35% absorbed vs 2-20% for non-heme plant iron. The most bioavailable iron source in the human diet.

Goitrogens

Substances that suppress thyroid function by interfering with iodine uptake or thyroid hormone synthesis. Found in cruciferous vegetables and soy.

Glucosinolates

Sulfur-containing compounds in cruciferous vegetables that can interfere with thyroid hormone synthesis by inhibiting iodine uptake.

Phytoestrogens

Plant-derived compounds that mimic estrogen in the body. Can disrupt hormonal balance, affect fertility, and interfere with endocrine function. Found in soy and flaxseed.

Cyanogenic Glycosides

Plant compounds that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when plant tissue is damaged. Found in cassava, bamboo shoots, and lima beans. Can cause convulsions and death.

Saponins

Plant glycosides that produce foam when dissolved in water. Can disrupt cell membranes and lyse (destroy) red blood cells. Found in legumes and quinoa.

Hyperpalatable

Engineered foods designed to maximize pleasure response beyond anything found in nature. Combine sugar, salt, fat, and artificial flavors to override natural satiety signals.

FODMAPs

Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides And Polyols — short-chain carbohydrates poorly absorbed in the small intestine, causing digestive distress.

IBS

Irritable Bowel Syndrome — a chronic functional GI disorder characterized by abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits. Often worsened by high-fibre diets.

Retinol

The active, preformed version of vitamin A found only in animal foods (liver, eggs, dairy). Immediately usable by the body, unlike beta-carotene from plants.

CoQ10

Coenzyme Q10 — essential for mitochondrial energy production. Concentrated in heart muscle. Levels decline with age and statin drug use.

Methylation

A biochemical process involving the transfer of methyl groups. Essential for DNA synthesis and repair, detoxification, neurotransmitter production, and gene expression regulation.

EPA/DHA

Eicosapentaenoic acid and Docosahexaenoic acid — long-chain omega-3 fatty acids found primarily in fish and seafood. Essential for brain, heart, and immune function.

Haemolysis

The rupturing of red blood cells, releasing hemoglobin into the bloodstream. Can be caused by saponins, certain toxins, and other membrane-disrupting compounds.

Cytotoxicity

The quality of being toxic to cells. Cytotoxic substances can damage or kill cells through various mechanisms including membrane disruption and mitochondrial damage.

Chelation

The process by which a molecule binds to a metal ion, forming a complex. Phytates and oxalates chelate essential minerals, preventing their absorption.

Hyperkalemia

Dangerously elevated potassium levels in the blood. Can cause cardiac arrhythmias and cardiac arrest. Risk factor with potassium supplementation.

Half-life

The time required for half of a substance to be eliminated from the body. Longer half-life means greater accumulation risk with repeated exposure.

Seed Oils

Oils extracted from plant seeds using industrial processes (hexane solvent, high heat, chemical deodorization). Includes soybean, canola, sunflower, corn, and cottonseed oils.

Acrylamide

A chemical compound formed when starchy foods are heated above ~120°C through the Maillard reaction. IARC Group 2A probable carcinogen. Also a confirmed neurotoxin.

Maillard Reaction

A chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars at high temperatures. Responsible for browning and flavor development — but also acrylamide formation.

Glycidamide

A metabolite of acrylamide formed in the body. More reactive than acrylamide — binds directly to DNA forming adducts that can cause mutations.

Circadian Rhythm

The ~24-hour internal clock governing sleep-wake cycles, hormone release, body temperature, and metabolism. Synchronized primarily by light exposure through the eyes.

Linoleic Acid

An omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid (18:2n-6). The primary fatty acid in seed oils. Incorporates into cell membranes and drives chronic inflammation when consumed in excess.

Ruminant

An animal with a specialized four-chamber stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum) that ferments plant matter before digestion. Includes cattle, sheep, goats, bison, and deer.

Saturated Fat

A fatty acid with no double bonds in its carbon chain. All carbon atoms are "saturated" with hydrogen. Straight chain, packs tightly, solid at room temperature, highly resistant to oxidation.

Monounsaturated Fat

A fatty acid with one double bond in its carbon chain, creating a single kink. Liquid at room temperature, moderately stable. Found in olive oil, avocados, and animal fats.

Polyunsaturated Fat (PUFA)

A fatty acid with multiple double bonds, creating multiple kinks. Always liquid, highly prone to oxidation and rancidity. Dominant in industrial seed oils. Includes omega-6 and omega-3 fats.

Oxidation

A chemical reaction where a substance loses electrons, often by reacting with oxygen. In fats, oxidation creates toxic aldehydes and other harmful compounds. PUFAs are most vulnerable.

Cortisol

A steroid hormone produced by the adrenal glands. Follows a circadian pattern — peaks in early morning for alertness, declines throughout the day. Chronic elevation drives inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.

Melatonin

A hormone produced by the pineal gland in response to darkness. Regulates sleep-wake cycles, has antioxidant properties. Production is suppressed by light exposure, especially blue light at night.

Glymphatic System

A macroscopic waste clearance system that utilizes a unique system of perivascular tunnels to promote efficient elimination of soluble proteins and metabolites from the central nervous system.

Xenoestrogens

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals found in plastics (like BPA and phthalates) that mimic estrogen in the body.

Adenosine

A chemical in the brain that builds up during the day to signal sleepiness. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, preventing this signal.

Neurotoxin

A chemical that interferes with the normal function of the nervous system. Caffeine acts as a neurotoxin by blocking adenosine receptors.

Collagen

The main structural protein in the extracellular matrix found in the body's various connective tissues. Abundant in bone broth and skin.

Glycine

An amino acid that acts as a neurotransmitter and is a key component of collagen. Supports sleep, liver detox, and gut health.

Autophagy

The body's way of cleaning out damaged cells, in order to regenerate newer, healthier cells. Triggered by fasting.

Metabolic Flexibility

The capacity for the organism to adapt fuel oxidation to fuel availability. The ability to switch between burning carbs and fat efficiently.
